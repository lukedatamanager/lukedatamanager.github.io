Imagine walking into the world’s largest library. It’s five stories tall and filled with millions of books. Together, they form the most extensive archive of human knowledge ever assembled; a treasure trove capable of reshaping how we see the world and, more importantly, how we act within it. Economic trends that could motivate us to deliver a fairer future, health statistics that might save lives, and detailed and widespread measurements of our changing planet—from the melting of glaciers to the decline of biodiversity.

You and a team of your choosing have been assigned a crucial and timely task: to synthesise all this information into a series of reports for international leaders in politics and industry—those with the most power to change the world—and provide recommendations on how to proceed. The future of the world depends on it.

But here’s the catch.

- There is no catalogue. The books are organised by topic, author, or relevance, or retrievable based on keywords. They’re just… cluttered on the shelves like a shuffled deck of cards.
- The books are written in dozens of languages, some of which you’ve never seen before. A few are in code, others in dense academic jargon.
- Some books are held in glass cages. You can see them, but must pay a premium to access them.
- The ones you can access are scattered across different rooms, with no clear path between them. To answer even a simple question, you’d need to hunt through hundreds of books, piece together fragments of information, and hope you’re not missing something critical.

Now, imagine that this library is the internet—and the books are data. That’s the world we’re living in today.

## The data are there, so why aren’t we making use of them?

There is an overwhelming scientific consensus that the planet is burning, and that human emissions of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases are the cause. We have lost half of the world’s wild areas, and biodiversity is collapsing at an unprecedented rate. Water use has skyrocketed, with aquifers depleting faster than they can replenish. And yet, we continue with little or no change, often compounding the problem further as total carbon emissions and water use continue to rise each year.

At the same time, fake news spreads across social media as fast as wildfires across our increasingly parched landscapes, its messages echoed and amplified by grossly irresponsible world leaders who should know better.

What frustrates me most is that we already have the data to debunk many of these dubious and dangerous claims. It should not matter where along the political spectrum you are. It should be possible to establish a common ground truth that we can all share, that we can democratically make informed decisions upon. Data can of course be used out of context to mislead, but the data themselves are not political, and have no agenda. 

We need a data ecosystem that facilitates fast sharing of data. That describes their original context. That enables anyone—not just experts—to quickly access, analyse, and understand data, and come to their own conclusions.

## How do we put data into the hands of those who need it?

What if you could instantly access all the sea surface temperature measurements for a given month and region—from satellites, research ships, ocean buoys, and coastal stations—with just a few clicks? What if the data you needed to make critical decisions weren’t scattered across a hundred different data repositories, but unified, standardised, and ready to use?

Questions like this motivate me in my job as a scientific data manager. For some, data management can be a dry or tedious topic. I am going to try and convince you that it is exciting and powerful. When done well, it transforms published data from a static archive into a living resource, enabling widespread reuse beyond its original context. This reuse can fuel further research, drive data-driven decisions, and create real societal impact.

Making this vision a reality is not simply a matter of collecting more data. We already produce extraordinary amounts of it. The challenge is to make those data easy to find, easy to access, easy to combine, and easy to understand—regardless of who collected them or where they are stored. Achieving this requires more than better technology. It requires shared standards, common ways of describing data, and systems that allow independent collections to work together as though they were part of a single, interconnected whole. These are the foundations of modern scientific data management. 

Throughout my blog posts, I will introduce how to make data more reusable, more trustworthy, and ultimately more useful. Not only for researchers, but for policymakers, businesses, educators, and anyone who wants to make decisions grounded in evidence.

Of course, improved data management alone is not enough to bring about the revolutionary changes we need. We are faced with multifaceted, complex issues that demand systemic shifts in policy, culture, and technology. But data are the foundation upon which information, knowledge and wisdom are built. Good data management acts as a catalyst, amplifying the impact of data and—hopefully—enabling us to extract more wisdom from it.

And let’s be honest: the world could use a little more wisdom right now.
